Redesigns

How to redesign your website without losing your Google rankings

By Zach Wennstedt · Published October 9, 2026 · 7 min read

Direct answer: A redesign only costs you rankings when Google loses track of your pages or your best content disappears. List every current URL, keep or 301-redirect each one, carry over the content and titles that already bring in visits, and check Search Console in the weeks after launch. Do those four things and a new design is usually neutral or positive for search.

Why redesigns lose rankings

Google does not rank a design. It ranks pages: their addresses, their words, their titles and how fast and usable they are. When a business owner says "we got a new website and our calls dropped," the cause is almost always one of a short list: page addresses changed with no redirects, pages that brought in traffic were deleted or merged, the copy was trimmed to look cleaner, title tags were replaced with generic ones, or the new site is slower on phones. Every one of those is preventable.

Before you touch the design

1. Inventory every URL. Export a list of every page on the current site. Your sitemap.xml is a good start, and Google Search Console (Indexing, then Pages) shows what Google actually has on file. Include PDFs and image-heavy pages if people find them in search.

2. Find the pages that earn their keep. In Search Console, open the Performance report, switch to Pages and sort by clicks for the last 12 months. Those pages are the ones you cannot afford to lose. Write down their titles, headings and the search terms that bring visitors in.

3. Save a copy of the old site. Keep a full backup or a crawl export with titles, meta descriptions and headings for every page. If something drops later, you need to be able to compare before and after.

4. Record your baseline. Note monthly calls, form fills and Search Console clicks before launch. Without a baseline you cannot tell a real drop from normal seasonal swings, and Colorado Springs businesses do have seasons, from summer tourism to the PCS moving rush.

During the build

5. Keep URLs where you can. The safest redirect is the one you never need. If /roof-repair/ ranks today, keep that address on the new site.

6. Map every changed URL to a 301 redirect. For each old address that changes, pick the closest matching new page and set a permanent (301) redirect. Do not send everything to the home page; Google tends to treat that like a deleted page.

7. Carry the content over. A cleaner layout is fine. Cutting a 900-word service page down to two sentences is not. Keep the substance, the local details and the answers people search for, and improve them while you are in there.

8. Keep titles and headings close to what works. If a page ranks for "furnace repair Colorado Springs," the new title and H1 should still say so.

9. Build for phones first. Google indexes the mobile version of your site. Test the new design on a real phone over cellular data, not only on a desktop monitor. Our free website speed test is a quick first check.

Launch day

10. Remove any "noindex" left over from the staging site. This single setting has sunk more launches than anything else on this list. Check the robots meta tag and robots.txt.

11. Test the redirects. Click through your old URL list and confirm each one lands on the right new page with a 301, not a 302 or a 404.

12. Update and submit the sitemap. Make sure sitemap.xml lists only the new, live URLs, then submit it in Search Console.

13. Check tracking. Confirm analytics, call tracking and form notifications all fire. Send a test lead through every form.

14. Update your listings. If any page addresses changed, update the links in your Google Business Profile, social profiles and directory listings.

The first month after launch

Watch the Search Console Pages report for new 404 errors and fix them with redirects as they appear. Compare clicks on your top pages against your baseline. Some movement in the first few weeks is normal while Google recrawls the site. A steady, deep drop on specific pages usually points back to a missing redirect or lost content on those pages, which you can now spot because you kept the old copy.

Quick answers

Will a website redesign hurt my Google rankings?

It can, but it does not have to. Most ranking drops after a redesign come from changed URLs without 301 redirects, deleted pages that used to bring in traffic, or content that was cut down. Keep or redirect every URL and keep the content that earns visits, and a redesign is usually neutral or positive.

What is a 301 redirect and why does it matter?

A 301 redirect is a permanent forward from an old web address to a new one. It sends visitors and search engines to the right page instead of a 404 error, and it tells Google the page has moved so the old page's standing can carry over to the new address.

How long does it take Google to settle after a redesign?

It varies by site size and how often Google crawls you. Small sites often see Google pick up the changes within a few days to a few weeks. Submitting the new sitemap in Google Search Console and fixing any crawl errors quickly helps.

Should I change my domain name during a redesign?

Only if you have a strong reason. A domain change adds a second layer of risk on top of the redesign. If you must move, do it with page-to-page 301 redirects and use the Change of Address tool in Google Search Console.
